BlueCrest and the limits of significant influence

The Supreme Court has held that an LLP member’s influence counts for the salaried member rules only if it is rooted in the members’ legal rights. Commercial importance is not enough.

Edward Brown KC·23 October 2026

A highly paid portfolio manager in an LLP may still be taxed as an employee. That is the practical effect of the Supreme Court’s decision in HMRC v BlueCrest Capital Management (UK) LLP [2026] UKSC 18, handed down on 1 July 2026.

The case concerned the salaried member rules. A member of an LLP is treated as an employee for income tax purposes where three conditions are met. Broadly, the member receives disguised salary (Condition A), lacks significant influence over the affairs of the partnership (Condition B) and has made an insufficient capital contribution (Condition C). Fund managers and professional services firms commonly pay senior people through an LLP, and Condition B is usually where the argument lies.

Influence must have a legal source

The Court held that influence counts for Condition B only if it can be traced to the legally enforceable rights and duties of the members, and of the partnership and its members.

It need not come from voting rights. An appointment, or authority delegated under the governing arrangements, will do, provided that it is rooted in those rights. Influence with no legal source does not count.

Commercial importance is therefore not enough. The Court said in terms that it is not sufficient that a member has a significant impact on profits because he or she is an excellent rainmaker or investor. A portfolio manager with a large book and wide investment discretion may still lack significant influence.

The effect is that Condition B is more readily satisfied. More members will lack significant influence, and so will be within the rules if the other two conditions are also met.

Desk-based allocations

On Condition A, allocations based on the profits generated by an individual or a desk were disguised salary, even though the total was capped by reference to the partnership’s overall profits. Remuneration that is fixed, or that varies without reference to the overall profits or losses of the partnership, may satisfy the condition.

What remains open

The Court remitted the application of Condition B to the individual members to the First-tier Tribunal. The decision settles the test, not the status of the members concerned.

Consequences

A member who meets all three conditions is treated as an employee for income tax purposes. PAYE applies and, under separate regulations, Class 1 National Insurance contributions are due. The rules govern tax status only. Whether a member is an employee or worker for the purposes of employment legislation is a separate question, decided under the employment-law tests and the Limited Liability Partnerships Act 2000.

After BlueCrest, the question is not whether a member matters to the business, but whether the members’ agreement says so.

This post states the law as at 23 October 2026.
